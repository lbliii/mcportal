# Fold the room into an edition

MCPortal's room should stop presenting every portal as an equal, side-scrolling column and start presenting itself as a finite **front page**. That means one lead story, a few agent-picked highlights, and compact portal blocks in a stable order, all sized to fit the chat without internal scrolling. The multi-column lane belongs in fullscreen. Fifteen years of products and HCI research agree on this. Every product that made a dense or novel spatial layout its default later demoted it to an opt-in: TweetDeck columns, Mastodon's multi-column UI, Flipboard's page flip, Windows Live Tiles. Meanwhile, the readers that survived let users choose density, often per source (Reddit, Feedly). The research identifies the current inline design (a fixed 560px lane scrolling horizontally, with 18rem columns that each scroll vertically, inside a vertically scrolling chat) as close to a worst case. It nests scrolling two levels deep on two axes, horizontal scrolling goes undiscovered on desktop, and OpenAI's Apps SDK explicitly bans nested scrolling in inline cards. The fix also opens a way to feel next-generation. Size and position carry editorial weight, as on a newspaper front page. The user's agent supplies the ranking and the "why this" line, so the hierarchy is legible rather than a black box. Discrete semantic zoom (room, then portal, then item), animated with now-Baseline View Transitions, replaces horizontal panning. Two parts of this lack direct evidence: no controlled study tests bento grids or front-page layouts, and none tests layouts at chat-embedded widths. Those recommendations are reasoned extrapolation from adjacent findings, not measured results.

## Dense layouts win loyalists and lose defaults

The multi-column dashboard has a long history, and it is a history of retreat. TweetDeck was the archetype: "a series of customizable columns" for timelines, lists, searches and users, used mainly by professionals and media staff ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TweetDeck)). It lost its mobile apps in 2013 and its desktop apps by 2022, and by March 2026 it sat behind X's **Premium+ tier at roughly $40/month** ([PiunikaWeb](https://piunikaweb.com/2026/03/27/x-pro-tweetdeck-locked-premium-plus-new-product-coming/)). Mastodon launched with TweetDeck-style columns as its default. In **version 2.9 (2019)** it switched new users to a single column and kept multi-column as the opt-in "Advanced Web Interface" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastodon_(social_network))). Flipboard's page flip was "the heart" of the 2010 iPad app ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-old-magazines-and-lamborghinis-inform-flipboards-design-process-and-approach/)), but on the web it read as out of step with native scrolling and gave way. Windows 11 removed Live Tiles, and press accounts said few users ever customized them ([Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/news/windows-11-heres-all-the-features-microsoft-just-killed)). In each case the distinctive layout delighted a minority, and the default became something simpler.

Start pages failed for a related reason: they made users do the work. Pageflakes shut down in 2012 and iGoogle in 2013. Both required users to choose, arrange and prune widgets themselves ([Wikipedia: Pageflakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pageflakes); [Failory](https://www.failory.com/google/igoogle)). Google Reader's "devoted following" ([Google Reader blog](http://googlereader.blogspot.com/2013/03/powering-down-google-reader.html)) proved how quickly that minority moves: Feedly gained **3 million users in two weeks** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Reader)). For MCPortal the lesson cuts both ways. The agent building and arranging the room solves the maintenance burden that killed start pages. But a room the user never maintains has to stay legible on its own, which means seen-state, collapsed noisy portals, and an obvious place to start reading.

The products that lasted resolved the density conflict by letting users choose. Reddit's 2018 redesign shipped **Card, Classic and Compact** views because no single density served both newcomers and scanners ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/reddit-redesign-launches-to-first-users/)). Old Reddit still drew an estimated **84.8M visits in August 2026**, about 8% of reddit.com's volume ([Semrush](https://www.semrush.com/website/old.reddit.com/overview/)). Feedly goes a step further and sets Title-only, Magazine or Cards **per source** ([Feedly Docs](https://docs.feedly.com/article/276-how-do-i-change-the-views-of-my-feeds-and-source)): title-only for high-volume feeds, cards for visual ones. Tapestry (2025) went the other direction, toward unified chronological feeds with "Muffle," which collapses noise instead of hiding it ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/04/team-behind-twitterrific-launches-a-multi-feed-app-called-tapestry/)). The fair reading of the evidence is a hybrid: a simple, single-stream default; per-source columns as a power mode; and density chosen per portal.

Users also defend two things: chronology and the unit of meaning. Pinterest's switch to a best-first "smart feed" raised repinners **more than 20%** ([Pinterest Engineering](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/pinnability-machine-learning-in-the-home-feed-64be2074bf60)), yet users pushed hard enough that a chronological Following feed came back. Tumblr's 2017 "Best Stuff First" shipped on by default, and staff later promised they were "not getting rid of the reverse-chronological dashboard" ([Tumblr staff](https://www.tumblr.com/staff/722502027705565184/hi-folks-there-seems-to-be-a-lot-of)). Tumblr's March 2026 change, which split reblog notes per contributor, drew **more than 100,000 complaints** and was reversed within a day ([PiunikaWeb](https://piunikaweb.com/2026/03/18/tumblr-reverses-reblog-notes-update-backlash/)). In MCPortal, the equivalent units are the portal and each item's provenance. Agent curation can sit on top of them, but it should never replace or hide them.

## Two scroll axes inside a third is the worst case

The research is clearest on the current inline design. NN/g finds that desktop users don't expect horizontal scrolling and that "even strong cues such as arrows frequently remain unnoticed." It recommends always offering an alternative path, showing progress, and keeping arrows visible ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-scrolling/)). Nested scroll regions cost keyboard users start/end and page jumps and create scroll traps. Accessibility guidance says to limit their number, delineate them clearly, and capture the wheel only after the user clicks in ([Access-Ability](https://buttondown.com/access-ability/archive/nested-scroll-bars-are-the-one-of-the-biggest/)). `overscroll-behavior: contain` stops scroll chaining, but only inside an actual scroll container ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/Properties/overscroll-behavior)). In the chat, that means a wheel gesture over a column moves the column rather than the conversation, and a reader trying to scroll past the room gets stuck. OpenAI's Apps SDK rules are blunt: inline cards should have **"No nested scrolling,"** should auto-fit their content, should avoid "deep navigation or multiple views within a card," and carousels should hold **3–8 items**. Fullscreen is reserved for "rich tasks that cannot be reduced to a single card" ([OpenAI Apps SDK](https://developers.openai.com/apps-sdk/concepts/ui-guidelines)). The current lane breaks the no-nested-scrolling rule twice.

The MCP Apps spec already provides the way out. Hosts can declare flexible dimensions, such as `{ "width": 400, "maxHeight": 600 }`. The view sizes itself to its content, the SDK reports size changes automatically, and the view can call `ui/request-display-mode` to move into `fullscreen` ([MCP ext-apps spec](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/ext-apps/blob/main/specification/2026-01-26/apps.mdx)). An inline room should therefore be an **auto-height, non-scrolling summary** that pages ("next 5") instead of scrolling, and it should treat fullscreen as the place for the scroll-heavy lane. Attention data reinforces the point. In NN/g's 2018 eyetracking study, **57% of viewing time went to the first screenful** and **74% to the first two** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). In a roughly 600px inline frame, the first screenful is nearly everything a reader closely examines, so it should hold the edition's most important items rather than the first few rows of whichever columns happen to fit.

Density has a firm floor. WCAG 2.2 AA requires **24×24 CSS px** targets (or 24px spacing), AAA asks for 44×44, Apple specifies 44pt, and Material 48dp ([wcag22aa.org](https://wcag22aa.org/new-criteria/target-size/)). Material's density scale removes **4dp per step** but keeps touch targets at their minimum hit area at every density ([Material](https://m2.material.io/design/layout/applying-density.html)). Compact rows of 32–36px are fine inline as long as each row is a full-width hit area of about 44px. MCPortal's existing `--mp-control-minimum: 24px` and `--mp-control-touch: 44px` tokens already encode this.

## Lists for reading, size for importance, stable places for memory

For text, a 1D list beats a 2D grid. Bederson notes that people "are not as good at scanning 2D designs as 1D layouts – unless the layout is highly structured" ([Bederson 2011](http://www.cs.umd.edu/~bederson/images/pubs_pdfs/2011_-_BIT_-_Promise_of_ZUIs.pdf)). NN/g found that mobile image grids showed only **about 4 items per screen**. One needed four screen-lengths of scrolling where a competitor's text list needed one, and one user looked at just **2 of 42** categories before choosing ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/image-vs-list-mobile-navigation/)). Images earn their space only when items differ visually. Pinterest's grid works because its cards are images and the task is recognition. Pinterest still removed descriptions from feed cards and moved them to closeups ([Tailwind](https://www.tailwindapp.com/blog/what-you-need-to-know-about-pinterests-activity-changes)). Its engineers also concluded that the plain grid "limits our ability to provide more context," so they added modules placed by **skip-slot blending**: a module takes a slot only when it beats the pin it displaces, and modules users ignore get fatigued ([Pinterest Engineering](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/module-relevance-on-homefeed-ae76f8b545b2)). Masonry carries an accessibility cost. In `grid-lanes` (now in Safari 26.4), visual order diverges from DOM order, which likely fails WCAG 2.4.3 Focus Order ([Matuzović](https://matuzo.at/blog/2026/grid-lanes-accessibility)). Bento grids raise the same concern under SC 1.3.2 ([Inkbot Design](https://inkbotdesign.com/bento-grid-design/)).

Hierarchy is what lets readers skim efficiently. NN/g calls the layer-cake pattern, where the eye jumps from heading to heading, "by far the most effective way to scan pages." Without distinct headings, readers fall back on the weaker F-pattern ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). Bento grids apply this at the layout level: tiles of mixed size, scanned largest first. Practitioners, though, warn that bento "do[es] not work for data-dense product dashboards" because asymmetry conflicts with uniform scanning ([rajeshrnair](https://rajeshrnair.com/blog/design/ui-ux/ui-design-trends-2026-bento-grids-glassmorphism.html)). No rigorous usability study of bento exists. The defensible synthesis is to apply size variation **between** items at the top level (lead, highlights, portals) and keep lists uniform **within** a portal. Fixed size tiers, like iOS widgets' small, medium and large ([Adapptor](https://www.adapptor.com.au/blog/designing-for-ios-14-home-screen-widgets)), make size a readable signal rather than free-form decoration.

Spatial memory rules out letting importance move things around. In Findlater and McGrenere's study, static split menus were **significantly faster** than adaptive menus that reordered by use, and user-adaptable menus were the most preferred ([Findlater & McGrenere 2004](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/985692.985704)). NN/g's guidance says spatial memory is "fuzzy," needs a stable UI plus repetition, and holds up better when a layout scales than when it reflows ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/spatial-memory/)). Bederson's rule is that "once an object is placed in space, it should not move." This sets up a real tension with an edition whose lead story changes daily. The resolution is to split the page into two zones. A **masthead zone** changes every edition and is the agent's to fill. A **portal zone** keeps a fixed, user-adaptable order and signals change through badges and counts, never by moving portals around.

## Discrete semantic zoom beats panning a text canvas

Zoomable interfaces succeeded narrowly (maps, photos) and failed as a general organizing principle. Bederson lists the reasons: a heavy short-term memory load, "desert fog," motion sickness in some people, and a blunt verdict on content type: "Photos are good, purely textual documents are bad" ([Bederson 2011](http://www.cs.umd.edu/~bederson/images/pubs_pdfs/2011_-_BIT_-_Promise_of_ZUIs.pdf)). His guidelines map onto a reading room closely. Use breadth over depth. Use a scannable grid. Keep consistent placement. Keep each object's bounding box constant across semantic zoom levels. Use constrained navigation (click to zoom in, a button to zoom out), which makes it "impossible to get lost." The best-matching result is Cockburn, Gutwin and Alexander's space-filling thumbnails. With only two levels, a fully zoomed-out view of stable tiles and a fully zoomed-in reader, users "significantly outperform[ed]" panning, zooming and overview+detail designs, particularly when revisiting ([Cockburn et al. 2008 survey](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/cockburn-surveys08.pdf)). The same survey warns that abrupt level changes force users to reorient, and that tuned animation "can dramatically reduce the cognitive load." Klein and Bederson found that a 500ms scroll animation cut reading errors by **54%**.

Same-document View Transitions became **Baseline with Firefox 144 in October 2025** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/same-document-view-transitions-are-now-baseline-newly-available)). That makes animated discrete zoom cheap inside an iframe. A shared `view-transition-name` on a portal tile or headline lets it grow into the next level ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/view-transitions)). Two cautions apply. Nobody has verified how host sandboxes treat the API, so feature-detect and fall back to an instant swap. And WCAG 2.3.3 calls for honoring `prefers-reduced-motion` for zoom-type motion ([Stark](https://www.getstark.co/wcag-explained/operable/seizures-and-physical-reactions/animation-from-interactions/)).

## Editions with legible agent ranking feel next-generation

The 2024–2026 products that feel current share three traits: they are finite, they synthesize, and they show their work. Kagi News publishes once a day and promises "everything important in just five minutes. No endless scrolling," framing news as "a contained ritual" ([Kagi](https://blog.kagi.com/kagi-news)). Readwise's Daily Digest serves "an appetizer of new items followed by a main course of previously saved items" ([Readwise](https://blog.readwise.io/the-next-chapter-of-reader-public-beta/)). Google Discover's AI summaries stack the cited publishers' logos on the card ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/15/google-discover-adds-ai-summaries-threatening-publishers-with-further-traffic-declines)). Threads' "Dear Algo" lets users steer the feed in plain language. The effect expires after three days, and affected posts carry a "why you're seeing this" label ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/11/threads-new-dear-algo-ai-feature-lets-you-personalize-your-feed/)). Bluesky's feed generators return only an ordered list of post IDs, which the client then hydrates ([Bluesky docs](https://docs.bsky.app/docs/starter-templates/custom-feeds)). That is exactly the split MCPortal needs: the agent returns ranked IDs and reasons, and the server renders them. Artifact is the counter-example. Repeated pivots muddied its purpose, and downloads fell to about **12K a month** before it shut down ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/18/why-artifact-from-instagrams-founders-failed-shut-down/)). AI ranking is table stakes, not a moat, and a sprawling feature set erodes it.

On visual direction, current editorial trends favor type-led hierarchy, serif headlines, disciplined grids and restrained palettes ([Tubik](https://tubikstudio.com/blog/media-editorial-website-design/)). These sources are trend commentary rather than research, but they fit MCPortal's vintage sci-fi print direction. A newspaper front page *is* a bento grid in which size and position encode editorial weight. Read as an edition rather than a dashboard, the room gets a masthead, an issue number and a defined end. That is what makes it feel fresh instead of like a retro skin on TweetDeck. A tension remains unresolved here. Kagi deliberately rejects personalization to avoid filter bubbles, while an agent-ranked room is personalization taken to the limit. A standing "from outside your usual" slot is a cheap hedge.

## Implications for MCPortal

**Hierarchy signals.** Use a three-tier hierarchy, encoded the same way everywhere and never by moving portals. Unseen items (from the existing per-portal seen sets) get a weight change plus a small mark, and each portal header shows a "N new" count. Agent picks from `show_highlights` get a **"why this" line** in the agent's own words plus the source mark, as Discover and Dear Algo do. MCPortal never calls a model, so the reasons arrive with the agent's ranked IDs, Bluesky-style. The **lead item** is the agent's single top pick, set in the largest type tier in the masthead. Without an agent call, fall back deterministically to the newest unseen item from the first pinned portal. Keep DOM order equal to rank order, so keyboard and screen-reader users get the same hierarchy. Keep "latest" one tap away in every portal as the chronology contract. Apply Pinterest's skip-slot rule: show a highlights strip only when there are picks, and suppress it when the user ignores it.

**Card shapes by content type.** Use one block-based item schema, in the spirit of Tumblr's NPF, with a small fixed set of shapes chosen per portal, as in Feedly:

| Shape | Use for | Inline footprint |
|---|---|---|
| Headline row | text/news/RSS, high volume | ~36px visual, 44px hit area; title first, source and time secondary |
| Lead | masthead top pick | kicker, large serif/Jost headline, 2-line "why this" |
| Picture tile | image/art/video portals | fixed aspect (cap like Pinterest's 2:3), heights settled before layout |
| Quote/post | social and short posts | text block, one level of quoting max (Tumblr's 2015 lesson) |
| Clip/saved | user's own clips | compact row with clip mark |

Settle heights before layout, as Gestalt Masonry requires ([Gestalt](https://github.com/pinterest/gestalt/blob/master/packages/gestalt/src/Masonry/README.md)), so the auto-height iframe doesn't jitter.

**A third layout: front page.** Add `frontpage` alongside `columns` and `shelves` and make it the inline default. Structure, top to bottom: a masthead (room name, edition date or number, "N new since last visit"); one lead; a highlights strip of at most 3 agent picks; then portal blocks in the user's fixed order, each showing a header, a count and its top 2–3 items in the portal's shape. Close with a colophon line ("You're caught up," plus "more in fullscreen"). Use two fixed size tiers, not free bento. At 380px everything is one column with no masonry. At 760px the portal blocks may form a strict two-column grid that preserves row-major DOM order. Keep `columns` as the fullscreen power layout, the TweetDeck/Mastodon pattern, and keep `shelves` for picture-led rooms.

**Semantic zoom levels.** Use three discrete levels with constrained navigation. Level 0 is the room front page: fixed tiles, names, counts, lead. Level 1 is a portal: a uniform list with "latest" and "highlights" toggles and paging. Level 2 is the item reader. Click to zoom in, and use one persistent back/zoom-out control in a fixed corner. Never offer a pannable canvas. Each portal tile keeps its bounding box and position at Level 0 across visits and widths. Wrap level changes in `document.startViewTransition` with shared names on the tile, headline and source mark, aim for 200–300ms, and fall back to an instant swap under reduced motion or missing support. Request fullscreen from Level 1 or 2 when a host supports it. Don't put a fullscreen button on every tile.

**Inline scroll ergonomics.** Retire the inline 560px lane. Inline, the room has **no internal scroll containers**. It auto-sizes to content within the host's `maxHeight` (around 600px per the spec example), and the outer chat is the only scroller. Overflow becomes paging inside a portal block ("next 5," with 44px controls) or a jump to Level 1, never a nested scroller. If a horizontal strip survives anywhere inline, such as the highlights strip on picture rooms, apply carousel rules: 3–8 items, always-visible arrows, a position indicator, keyboard support and scroll-snap, plus a non-horizontal alternate path. In fullscreen the current design becomes defensible. There, keep one scroll axis per region, keep `overscroll-behavior: contain` on columns, and add visible prev/next column controls, because horizontal discoverability stays poor even there. Because the change is large, ship it behind the existing layout setting and announce it. Pinterest's 2012 and Tumblr's 2026 backlashes both turned on silence.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the room as a publication with an editor, not a dashboard with widgets. Dashboards died because users had to be the editor. MCPortal's structural advantage is that the user's agent can play that role in chat, ranking, explaining and steering much as Dear Algo does, while the server stays model-free and renders whatever order it is handed. Expressing that through the grammar of a printed front page (masthead, lead, sized tiers, a colophon that says you're done) is what can make the room feel new rather than nostalgic. It also lines up with the art direction instead of fighting it.

The underexplored risk is the tension between a changing edition and stable spatial memory. The two-zone split (a volatile masthead over a fixed portal grid) is the best available answer, but it is untested. So is every width-specific recommendation here, since no study covers chat-embedded widgets. The cheapest high-value next step is to instrument it: track which zoom level users open, whether they request fullscreen, and how often "latest" overrides the agent's picks. Within a few weeks, that would show whether the front page works as a better default or only as a nicer-looking one.
